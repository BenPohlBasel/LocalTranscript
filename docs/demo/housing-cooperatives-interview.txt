Nora: Julian, you have been working with housing cooperatives for almost fifteen years now. What has actually changed in that time?
Julian: The biggest change is that cooperatives are no longer treated as a niche. Ten years ago we had to explain what a cooperative even was. Today cities come to us and ask how quickly we can deliver.
Nora: And is that pressure a good thing?
Julian: It cuts both ways. The demand is real, but speed is the enemy of participation. If a city wants four hundred flats in three years, there is very little room for the slow conversations that make a cooperative work.
Nora: That is exactly the tension I keep finding in the interviews. People describe the cooperative as a learning process, not as a delivery mechanism.
Julian: Precisely. The building is almost a by-product. What you are really producing is a group of people who have learned to decide things together.
Nora: Can you give me a concrete example?
Julian: In our last project the members spent nine months arguing about the shared kitchen. Nine months. From the outside that looks like a catastrophic delay. But those nine months are the reason the building still works twelve years later.
Nora: So the argument was the infrastructure.
Julian: You could put it that way, yes. The kitchen was just where the argument became visible.
Nora: Let me push on the transformation question. Cooperatives are often presented as a model for transforming the housing market as a whole. Do you believe that?
Julian: Honestly, no. Not as a model to be scaled. Cooperatives are very good at producing exceptions, and exceptions are valuable, because they show that another arrangement is possible. But an exception that is repeated four hundred times stops being an exception.
Nora: What would you want instead?
Julian: I would want the exception to change the rules. Procurement rules, land allocation, the way municipalities value a plot. That is where the transformation would actually happen. Not in the buildings themselves.
Nora: That is a much more modest claim than the one usually made.
Julian: It is more modest, and I think it is more honest. We are not going to solve the housing crisis with cooperatives. We might change what a city considers a reasonable thing to ask of a developer.
